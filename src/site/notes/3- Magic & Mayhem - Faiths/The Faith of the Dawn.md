*The Dawn Faith, known in its own tongue as Iiman Alfajr and to outsiders as the Faith of the Dawn, is the ancient religion of Nadira, and its holy book, the Naskaran or also known as the Eternal Flame, was written by the prophet Aknir al-Gemasud. It is a polytheistic faith centred on two deities, Shahrokh, the Sun God and Lord of Flames, and Yasamin, Goddess of the Moon and of dreams and death, and it holds that the world existed long before either of them, having passed through four ages and now awaiting a fifth. Its practice is built on five pillars: submission to the gods, prayer five times daily, charity of at least a portion of one's wealth to the less fortunate, fasting, and the pillar of self-improvement, alongside rites of worship that range from cremation upon an Eternal Flame to the reading of omens. The faith's symbols carry that duality through everything: the flame and the sun for Shahrokh, the moon and silver for Yasamin, the eclipse for their marriage, the serpent's petals for the apocalypse and for rebirth, and colours from black for the chitinous armour of the war god to gold and purple for royalty and the clergy.*

*Its mythology begins in the Age of Darkness, when only monsters wandered the starless dunes, and reaches its first crisis in the Age of Sun, when Shahrokh was born from a roaring flame and hunted the ancient beasts of the world. On a plateau of white salt he met the creature known only as the Serpent, which rose in a colossal explosion of salt, cast the first shadows over the dunes, and did not fall easily,, but fall it did at last, and from its flesh the god shaped humanity in his own image, from its bones raised the First City, and from its hide clothed his new people in golden robes before ruling them as the first Sultan. The age ended when whispers and heaviness came over him and he followed the scent of the serpent's blood into a deep cavern, where he found the last petal and eye of the beast and met Yasamin, who laid him into a deep sleep and took his realm for her own, beginning the Age of Night. Under her the rains came and the deserts turned to jungle, the souls of the dead were set in the sky as stars, and humanity was made mortal; Shahrokh, weakened, wandered among mortals, trained the first fire mages, and in time came to respect and love her. Their marriage began the Age of Dawn, in which the cycle of day and night was set, magic was given to humanity, and the two gods ascended, leaving behind the faith's central promise that what has risen may return.*

*Because the faith is built on that promise, its practices are shaped by both the dead and the end of days. The dead are cared for in two ceremonies, the Ausueud, held as quickly as possible in the home so as not to delay the soul on its way to Hukm, and the Salam, in which the body is carried to an Eternal Flame and given to the same fire that birthed the god, followed by fasting until the next dawn and a week of mourning. That care is urgent because the soul's journey can be distracted by the Whispers of the Serpent, and because the faith expects the Serpent to wake again: prophecy holds that the Age of Dawn will give way to a fifth era, the Age of Dusk, and the Naskaran records a chain of signs for it, necromancy spreading, the fire mages and stellar oracles bleeding from the eyes, the faithful turning hedonistic, stars flaring and dying, a storm of ash and salt quenching every Eternal Flame, monsters rising from the caves and the sea, the veil breaking so that the damned walk in service to their master and Hukm seeps into the mortal world, and at the last a child born with eyes of golden fire and a halo of silver, the final hope of humanity and the first sign of the weakened gods. When all of these come to pass, the Dusk will run its five millennia under the Serpent's wrath, until the prophesied one fulfils their destiny and Shahrokh and Yasamin descend once more with all their angels and warriors to end it.*
